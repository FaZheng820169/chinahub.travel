FAQ & Common Scams

Navigate cultural differences with confidence and learn how to spot tourist traps.

Cultural Differences & General FAQ

China is a fascinating country with thousands of years of continuous culture. Understanding these cultural nuances will make your trip smoother, more respectful, and far more enjoyable. Below are the most frequently asked questions by first-time visitors.

🚰 Can I drink the tap water?

Short answer: No. Tap water in China is generally not safe to drink straight from the faucet. While the water supply is treated at the municipal level, aging pipes and storage tanks in older buildings can introduce contaminants. Always drink bottled water or boil tap water thoroughly (for at least 3–5 minutes) before consuming. The good news: hotels in every price range provide electric kettles and complimentary bottled water daily. Many mid-range and luxury hotels also provide free drinking water dispensers on each floor. When in doubt, stick to sealed bottled water, which is cheap and available everywhere—convenience stores, street vendors, and vending machines.

💵 Should I leave a tip?

No. Tipping is not part of Chinese culture. In fact, leaving a tip can cause confusion or even embarrassment. In Chinese dining culture, the server may chase you down the street thinking you accidentally left money on the table! In high-end hotels, a service charge (typically 10–15%) is already included in your bill, so no additional tip is expected. The only exceptions are luxury tour guides or exceptional hotel concierge service, where a small token of appreciation (50–100 RMB) may be appropriate—but never feel obligated. In China, good service is considered a standard, not something extra to be rewarded.

🚽 Are squat toilets common?

Yes, especially in older buildings, train stations, public parks, and some tourist attractions. However, Western-style seated toilets are now standard in modern shopping malls, mid-range and luxury hotels, airports, and high-speed train stations. Crucial tip: Always carry your own pocket tissues and hand sanitizer, as toilet paper and soap are rarely provided in public restrooms—even in nice establishments. Many experienced travelers also recommend carrying a small portable seat cover and hand sanitizer. Airport and high-speed rail station restrooms are generally well-maintained with seated toilets, so plan accordingly during long journeys.

🗣️ Will people speak English?

English proficiency varies dramatically by city and context. In tier-1 cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen), many young professionals, hotel front-desk staff, and restaurant servers in international areas speak at least basic English. However, taxi drivers, shop owners in local neighborhoods, and folks in smaller cities generally do not speak English. Even in major cities, don't assume everyone you meet will understand you. Downloading a translation app before you arrive is absolutely essential. Google Translate works well if you have a VPN; alternatively, Baidu Translate (百度翻译) and Tencent TranSmart (腾讯翻译君) are excellent Chinese-developed apps that work without VPN and have high accuracy for Chinese-English translation, including photo translation for menus and signs.

🌡️ What's the best time to visit China?

The best times to visit China are spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October). During these months, temperatures are pleasant, air quality is generally better, and major attractions are less crowded than in summer. Summer (June–August) can be extremely hot and humid in southern and central China, while winter (December–February) is bitterly cold in the north but great for visiting Harbin's Ice Festival. Note that Chinese holidays—especially Chinese New Year (Spring Festival, usually January or February), National Day (October 1–7), and Labor Day (May 1–5)—cause massive domestic tourism surges. Transportation and hotels get extremely expensive, and popular sites are unbearably crowded. Plan accordingly or embrace the festive atmosphere if you don't mind the crowds.

🌙 Is it safe to walk at night?

Yes, China is one of the safest countries in the world for pedestrians at night. Violent crime rates are extremely low, and you'll often see people walking, exercising, or dining outdoors late into the evening. In major cities, central areas, shopping districts, and tourist zones are well-lit and patrolled. Women traveling alone generally report feeling safe walking at night in most Chinese cities. That said, use normal big-city precautions: avoid poorly lit alleys, keep your belongings secure, and stay aware of your surroundings. The biggest "danger" at night is often overzealous street vendors or fake "police" asking to see your passport—see our scams section below on how to handle the latter.

💳️ Can I use my credit card everywhere?

No—this is a common misconception. While credit card acceptance is growing in high-end hotels, luxury malls, and international chain restaurants, the vast majority of daily transactions in China happen via mobile payment apps (Alipay and WeChat Pay). Small restaurants, street vendors, local shops, and even some mid-range establishments do not accept credit cards at all. Before your trip, set up Alipay and/or WeChat Pay with an international credit or debit card (see our Payments & Apps guide for step-by-step instructions). Always carry some cash (Chinese Yuan/RMB) as a backup—at least 200–500 RMB—for places that can't process foreign cards.

😷 What's the deal with face masks?

Wearing face masks in public became deeply ingrained in Chinese culture after COVID-19, but the habit actually predates the pandemic. Many Chinese people wear masks when they have a cold (to protect others), when the air quality is poor, or simply as a personal comfort habit. As a tourist, you are not expected to wear a mask in daily situations. However, it's respectful to carry one and consider wearing it in crowded indoor spaces if you have a respiratory illness. Masks are still required in some medical facilities. Don't be surprised to see many locals wearing them—it's a cultural norm, not a cause for alarm.

📱 Do I need a VPN to access Google/Facebook/WhatsApp?

Yes. Google, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, YouTube, Twitter (X), and many other Western platforms are blocked in mainland China by the "Great Firewall." To access them, you'll need a reliable VPN (Virtual Private Network) installed and set up before you arrive in China—you cannot download most VPN apps from within China. We recommend subscribing to a reputable VPN service like ExpressVPN, NordVPN, or Astrill VPN and testing it before your departure. Note that VPN speeds can be slower in China, and the government periodically disrupts VPN traffic. For most travelers, using a VPN on your phone's mobile data (not Wi-Fi) provides the most stable connection. See our Internet & VPN guide for detailed recommendations.

🤝 Is bargaining expected?

Bargaining is expected and accepted in certain contexts: tourist markets, street stalls, small shops, and markets (like Beijing's Silk Market or Shanghai's Yuyuan Bazaar). A good rule of thumb: if there's no price tag, you can probably bargain. Start at 40–50% of the initial asking price and work your way up. Stay friendly and good-humored—bargaining in China is a social interaction, not a confrontation. However, never bargain in: department stores, supermarkets, restaurants with menus, or any shop with clearly marked prices. Also, avoid bargaining for tiny amounts—it's disrespectful. When in doubt, ask "可以便宜一点吗?" (kěyǐ piányí yīdiǎn ma? — "can you make it a little cheaper?") with a smile.

📸 Can I take photos everywhere?

Generally yes, but with important exceptions. Tourist attractions, street scenes, food, and landscapes are almost always fine to photograph. However, always ask permission before taking photos of people, especially in rural areas or of ethnic minority groups—some may find it intrusive. Strictly prohibited: taking photos of military personnel, government buildings, police stations, border crossings, or transportation infrastructure (airports, train stations from the inside). Doing so can get you detained or have your camera/phone confiscated. Museums and some temples may prohibit photography indoors—look for signs or ask staff. When in doubt, ask: "可以拍照吗?" (kěyǐ pāizhào ma? — "may I take a photo?").

🍜 Is the street food safe to eat?

Yes—with smart choices. China has an incredible street food culture, and millions of locals eat street food daily without issue. To minimize risk: choose stalls with high turnover (fresh ingredients, hot wok), watch the food being cooked, and avoid raw or undercooked meat. Popular night markets in cities like Chengdu, Xi'an, and Guangzhou are generally safe and an unforgettable culinary adventure. If you have a sensitive stomach, start with well-cooked foods (skewers, baozi, congyoubing) and avoid raw vegetables or unpeeled fruits from street vendors. Carry some anti-diarrhea medicine (like Imodium) just in case—it's available at any pharmacy (药店, yàodiàn) without prescription.

Common Tourist Scams to Avoid

China is one of the safest countries in the world when it comes to violent crime. However, tourists are occasionally targeted by specific financial scams in major tourist hubs (especially Beijing, Shanghai, and Xi'an). Being aware of these tactics will help you avoid them completely.

1. The "Tea House" or "Art Student" Scam

Where: Wangfujing (Beijing), The Bund / Nanjing Road (Shanghai), Near the Forbidden City

How it works: Friendly, well-dressed young locals (often claiming to be English students or art students) approach you to "practice their English." After a lovely 10-minute chat about your home country and their studies, they invite you to experience a "traditional tea ceremony" or visit an "art exhibition" nearby. Once inside the private venue, you are served tea, snacks, or "artworks." When the bill arrives, it's astronomically high—often hundreds or even thousands of dollars. Intimidating bouncers or "managers" will stand by the door and refuse to let you leave until you pay.

How to avoid: Politely decline all invitations from overly friendly strangers approaching you in tourist hotspots. Never follow anyone—no matter how charming—into a private establishment, teahouse, or "art gallery" you didn't originally plan to visit. If you want to experience a tea ceremony, go to a reputable, well-reviewed teahouse independently.

2. Fake Monks & "Buddhist Blessings"

Where: Near major temples (e.g., Lama Temple in Beijing, Yu Garden in Shanghai), tourist streets, and scenic areas

How it works: A person dressed as a Buddhist monk (usually in an orange or yellow robe) approaches you, smiles warmly, and hands you a shiny "golden amulet," a beaded bracelet, or a "peace and prosperity" card with Chinese characters. They may say a few English words of "blessing." As soon as you accept the item (or sometimes even if you don't), they pull out a notebook showing "donations" from other foreigners and aggressively demand money—often 100–500 RMB or more. They may follow you for several blocks if you try to walk away.

How to avoid: Real Buddhist monks in China do not beg for money on the street. They also do not hand out amulets to tourists. Simply ignore them completely—do not make eye contact, do not accept anything they hand to you, and keep walking. If they persist, a firm "不需要，谢谢" (bù xūyào, xièxie — "no need, thank you") while walking away is effective.

3. Illegal "Black Cabs" at Airports & Train Stations

Where: Arrivals halls at major airports (Beijing Capital, Shanghai Pudong, Guangzhou Baiyun) and train stations

How it works: Unlicensed touts wait near the arrivals gates, aggressively asking "Taxi? Taxi?" in broken English. They will lead you to an unmarked private car (a so-called "black cab") and quote you a flat fee that's often 3 to 5 times the actual metered price. Worse, some drivers may take a longer route, demand extra mid-journey, or drop you at the wrong location. These rides are also completely uninsured.

How to avoid: Ignore touts completely. Follow the official signs for the "Taxi Stand" (出租车) and wait in the official queue—these are metered, regulated, and safe. Even better, download DiDi (China's Uber) before your trip and set it up within Alipay or WeChat—you can book a ride in English and see the price upfront.

4. The "Good Samaritan" / Distraction Theft

Where: Tourist streets, markets, and subway stations in major cities

How it works: While you're looking at a map, taking a photo, or browsing in a market, a friendly person "accidentally" spills something on you (coffee, sauce, or water) or points out that something is on your bag/shoe. While you're distracted cleaning up, an accomplice picks your pocket or snatches your phone/wallet. Alternatively, someone may tap you on the shoulder to point out a "stain" on your back—while their partner steals from your open bag.

How to avoid: Keep your bag in front of you in crowded areas. If someone "accidentally" spills something on you, politely decline their help and move away immediately. Use a cross-body bag with zippers, and consider a money belt or hidden pouch for passports and large amounts of cash. Stay alert in crowds.

5. Fake Police Officers / "Document Check"

Where: Tourist areas in Beijing and Shanghai, occasionally on trains

How it works: A person in a police-style uniform (often without a clear badge number or official ID) approaches you on the street or in a tourist area and demands to see your passport. They may claim you've violated some regulation and threaten a fine or detention unless you pay an "on-the-spot penalty." Some scammers also work in pairs—one distracts you while the other checks your bag for "illegal items."

How to avoid: Real Chinese police will not ask for on-the-spot cash fines from tourists. If someone claiming to be police asks for your passport, ask to see their official police ID badge (警号, jǐnghào). You can also say "I need to call my embassy" or "Let's go to the police station together." Scammers will usually back off immediately. Always carry a photocopy of your passport instead of the original when sightseeing, and keep the original in your hotel safe.

6. Overcharging at "Tourist Menu" Restaurants

Where: Restaurants near major attractions (Forbidden City area, West Lake in Hangzhou, Li River in Guilin)

How it works: Restaurants near major tourist attractions may present you with a menu that has no prices—or they may swap your ordered (reasonably priced) dish for a much more expensive one when the bill comes. In seafood restaurants, they may weigh the food in front of you but use a rigged scale. Some places add "service charges" or "tea charges" that were never mentioned.

How to avoid: Before ordering, always confirm prices—ask "这个多少钱?" (zhège duōshǎo qián? — "how much is this?"). Better yet, eat at restaurants that display prices clearly on the menu or at the door. Use Google Maps or Dianping (大众点评, China's Yelp) to find well-reviewed restaurants away from major tourist sites. If you're unsure, ask your hotel concierge for recommendations.

7. The "Chess" or "Card Game" Scam in Parks

Where: Public parks and squares in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou

How it works: You'll see a group of friendly locals playing a card game or Chinese chess (围棋, wéiqí) in a park. They invite you to join or just watch. After a few friendly rounds, money enters the game. The rules suddenly become confusing, and you're told you've lost a significant amount. Alternatively, while you're focused on the game, an accomplice picks your pocket.

How to avoid: Enjoy watching locals play games in parks—it's a wonderful cultural experience—but never join a game where money is involved, especially if strangers invite you. If you want to learn Chinese chess or cards, do it in a structured setting (some cultural centers offer classes) rather than on a park bench with strangers.

8. Fake "Tourist Information Centers"

Where: Near major attractions, especially in Beijing and Xi'an

How it works: A shop or office near a tourist attraction displays signs that look official—"Tourist Information," "Visitor Center," or "Free City Maps." Once inside, they pressure you into buying overpriced tours, "exclusive" tickets, or memberships. Sometimes they even claim certain attractions are "closed today" and offer to take you to "better" (overpriced) alternatives.

How to avoid: Only trust official government-run tourist information centers, which are clearly marked with official signage and staffed by uniformed personnel. If someone approaches you on the street claiming an attraction is "closed," verify it yourself on Google Maps, the official website, or by asking your hotel. Never book tours or buy tickets from street touts.

Emergency Information & Useful Contacts

While China is very safe, it's always wise to be prepared. Save these numbers in your phone before you travel.

🚨 Emergency Numbers

  • Police (Public Security): 110
  • Fire Department: 119
  • Ambulance: 120
  • Traffic Accidents: 122
  • Foreigner Hotline (English): 12345 (press option for English in major cities)

🏥 Useful Contacts

  • Your Country's Embassy: Save the address and 24h emergency number
  • Travel Insurance: Save your policy number and emergency hotline
  • Hotel Front Desk: Save the hotel's business card (with Chinese address)
  • DiDi Customer Service: Available in-app
  • Alipay/WeChat Support: Available in-app (Chinese only)

💡 Pro Tip: Save a screenshot of your hotel's name and address in Chinese characters on your phone. If you get lost or need to show a taxi driver where you're going, you can simply show them the screenshot—no spoken Chinese required!

Download and set up these apps before you arrive in China. Many cannot be downloaded from within mainland China without a Chinese app store account.

Packing Essentials for China Travel

A well-packed bag can make the difference between a stressful trip and a smooth one. Here are the often-forgotten essentials.

🎒 Must-Have Items

  • ✅ Pocket tissues (纸巾) — carry them everywhere; public restrooms rarely have toilet paper
  • ✅ Hand sanitizer — for when soap isn't available
  • ✅ Power bank (充电宝) — you'll use your phone heavily for payments, maps, and translation
  • ✅ Universal power adapter — China uses Type A, I, and C sockets (220V)
  • ✅ VPN app — installed and tested before arrival
  • ✅ Comfortable walking shoes — you'll walk 10–20km a day in major cities
  • ✅ Face masks — useful in crowds and for air quality days

💊 Health & Medical

  • ✅ Anti-diarrhea medicine (e.g., Imodium) — available at Chinese pharmacies but bring your own
  • ✅ Motion sickness pills — if you're prone to car sickness (Chinese mountain roads!)
  • ✅ Sunscreen — UV can be strong, especially at high altitudes (Tibet, Sichuan)
  • ✅ Prescription medications — bring extra, plus the prescription label
  • ✅ Mosquito repellent — essential in southern China (Guangdong, Yunnan, Guangxi)
  • ✅ Thermal layers — if visiting northern China in winter (Harbin, Beijing in Dec–Feb)

⚠️ Note: Many medications that are over-the-counter in Western countries require a prescription in China. Bring a sufficient supply of any daily medications, along with a doctor's note in English (and ideally Chinese) explaining your condition.
