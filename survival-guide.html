Ultimate Survival Guide

Beyond payments and visas: everything you actually need to know to navigate China like a local. Getting a number, legal registration, essential apps, and staying safe.

📱 1. SIM Card & Chinese Phone Number (+86)

You absolutely need a local Chinese phone number (+86) for daily life. Everything in China requires SMS verification: logging into free Wi-Fi at malls, registering for ride-hailing apps, ordering food delivery, and even accessing some tourist attractions' online booking systems.

📍 Where to Buy a SIM Card

✅ Airport Kiosks (Easiest)

Major airports (Beijing Capital, Shanghai Pudong, Guangzhou Baiyun) have China Mobile and China Unicom kiosks in the arrivals hall. Open daily 6am–midnight. Bring your passport — real-name registration is mandatory. A typical tourist SIM: ¥99–¥199/month for 10–30GB data + some call minutes.

✅ Nihao Mobile (Best for Foreigners)

Nihao Mobile (你好移动) is a partnership between China Mobile and an international carrier. English customer service, accepts foreign credit cards online, and can be ordered before you fly and delivered to your hotel. Data plans: ¥99–¥299/month. Highly recommended for first-time visitors.

⚠️ Important: All SIM cards in China require real-name registration with your passport. You cannot buy an anonymous SIM. If your SIM is not registered, it will be deactivated within 24 hours.

⚖️ 2. Mandatory Police Registration (临时住宿登记表)

If you're staying in any accommodation other than a licensed hotel (Airbnb, friend's apartment, hostel), you MUST register with the local police within 24 hours of arrival. Failure to do so can result in fines (¥500–¥2000) or detention.

🏨 When the Hotel Does It For You

If you stay in a licensed hotel (any hotel with a business license), the front desk will handle police registration automatically. They will scan your passport and upload the information to the local police database. You do not need to visit the police station. Ask the front desk for a "Registration Form of Temporary Residence" (临时住宿登记表) as proof — some onward destinations (like Tibet or Xinjiang) may ask for it.

🏠 When YOU Must Register (Airbnb / Private)

If you're staying at an Airbnb, rental apartment, or a friend's place, you must go to the local police station (派出所, páichūsuǒ) within 24 hours. Bring: your passport, a copy of the lease/booking, and your host's ID. The process takes 10–20 minutes. The police will issue you a Temporary Residence Registration Form — keep this document with your passport at all times.

⚠️ Don't Skip This: If you overstay your visa or need to extend it, the first thing the PSB will ask for is your police registration form. No registration = big trouble. Also, some hotels will ask for your registration form if you check in for a second stay.

📱 3. Essential Survival Apps (Beyond Payments)

China's app ecosystem is massive. Here are the apps you'll actually use daily, beyond Alipay and WeChat.

🗺️ Maps: Apple Maps / Amap

Apple Maps uses Amap data but displays everything in English — a lifesaver for foreigners. Android users: download Amap (高德地图) — it has the most accurate real-time traffic in China.

🚕 DiDi (滴滴出行)

China's Uber. Book inside Alipay (search "DiDi") for an English interface. Much cheaper than taxis and safer (metered fare, driver rating).

🍜 Food: Meituan (美团)

Food delivery, restaurant reviews, and local services. English interface available inside the app. Delivery is incredibly cheap (¥5–¥15 delivery fee) and fast (30–45 minutes).

🚄 Trains: Railway 12306

Book high-speed train tickets via the "Railway 12306" mini-program inside Alipay/WeChat. You'll need your passport number. Tickets can be collected at the station or scanned directly from your phone (in some cities).

🎫 Entertainment: Damai (大麦)

Buy tickets for concerts, sports events, and tourist attractions. English interface available. Popular events sell out in seconds — set an alarm!

🏥 Healthcare: Guahao (挂号网)

Book doctor appointments at public hospitals. English-friendly international clinics are also listed. Always book ahead — walk-in wait times can be 4–8 hours at public hospitals.

🚄 4. Transportation Survival Guide

China has the world's largest high-speed rail network and efficient urban transport. Here's how to navigate it.

🚅 High-Speed Rail (G-Trains)

China's high-speed trains (G-series, 嘎嘎) run at 300–350 km/h and connect almost every major city. Book tickets via:

  • Alipay mini-program (search "Railway 12306") — English option available
  • Trip.com — English website, small booking fee
  • Station ticket window — bring your passport

💡 Pro Tip: Arrive at the station 30–45 minutes before departure. Security screening is similar to airport security. Your passport is your ticket — no paper needed if you have the QR code in the app.

🚇 Subway / Metro

All major Chinese cities have modern, clean subway systems. To pay: tap your Alipay/WeChat QR code at the turnstile (look for the "QR Code Entry" sign). Alternatively, buy a transit card at any station for ¥20–¥100 top-up. English signage is standard in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen.

🚕 Taxis vs. DiDi

Taxis are metered and legally required to use the meter. However, language barrier is a big issue — most drivers don't speak English. DiDi (China's Uber) is better: you enter your destination in English (or show it in Chinese), the fare is fixed upfront, and you pay via Alipay. No haggling, no language barrier, no scams.

🍜 5. Food & Water Safety Guide

China's food scene is incredible — but staying healthy requires smart choices. Here's what you need to know.

🚰 Can I Drink Tap Water? (Reminder)

No. Always drink bottled water or boiled water. Hotels provide kettles and complimentary bottled water daily. When in doubt, order boiled water (白开水, báikāishuǐ) at restaurants — it's free and safe.

🍢 Street Food: Is It Safe?

Yes — with smart choices. Choose stalls with high turnover (fresh ingredients, hot wok). Watch the food being cooked. Avoid raw or undercooked meat. Popular night markets in Chengdu, Xi'an, and Guangzhou are generally safe and unforgettable. Carry anti-diarrhea medicine (like Imodium) just in case.

🥢 Dining Etiquette Cheat Sheet

  • Wait to be seated — don't sit until the host assigns seats.
  • Don't stick chopsticks upright in rice — it resembles incense for the dead.
  • Tipping is not expected — see our FAQ for details.
  • Try everything — refusing food can be seen as rude. Just take a small portion.
  • Pay the bill by scanning the QR code on the table (via Alipay/WeChat).

🏥 6. Healthcare & Medical Information

China's healthcare system is vast. Here's how to navigate it as a foreigner.

🏥 International Clinics (Recommended)

For routine illnesses and non-emergencies, go to an international clinic or hospital in major cities. They have English-speaking doctors, Western medicine, and accept foreign insurance. Examples: Beijing United Family Hospital, Shanghai East International Medical Center, Guangzhou Clifford Hospital.

💊 Common Medications (Available at Pharmacies)

Many common medications are available over-the-counter at Chinese pharmacies (药店, yàodiàn) without prescription: anti-diarrhea medicine (蒙脱石散), cold medicine (感冒灵), and anti-inflammatory cream. Bring your own prescription medications with a doctor's note in English (and ideally Chinese).

🚨 Emergency: Dial 120 for ambulance. However, in major cities, it's often faster to take a DiDi to the nearest international hospital. Ambulances can be slow in traffic.

🎎 7. Cultural Etiquette (Don'ts)

✅ DO: Learn Basic Greetings

Learn "Nǐ hǎo" (你好, hello), "Xièxie" (谢谢, thank you), and "Zàijiàn" (再见, goodbye). Locals appreciate the effort and will often switch to English to help you.

❌ DON'T: Discuss Politics

Avoid discussing sensitive political topics with strangers or on social media while in China. This includes Taiwan, Tibet, Xinjiang, and criticism of the government. It can lead to complications.

✅ DO: Respect Elders

In Chinese culture, elders are highly respected. Offer your seat on public transport, let elders eat first at group meals, and address them with respectful titles.

❌ DON'T: Stick Chopsticks Upright

Never leave chopsticks sticking upright in a bowl of rice — it resembles incense burned for the dead and is considered very bad luck. Lay them flat across your bowl or on the table.

🌤️ 9. Seasonal Travel Tips for China

🌸 Spring (April–May)

Best time to visit most of China. Pleasant temperatures, flowers blooming, and relatively good air quality. Avoid May 1–5 (Labor Day holiday) — domestic tourism surges and prices spike.

☀️ Summer (June–August)

Hot and humid in central/southern China (30–40°C). Air quality is generally better due to summer rains. Best for: Tibet (clear skies), Inner Mongolia (grasslands), and escape to cooler regions like Guilin or Yunnan.

🍂 Autumn (September–October)

Another excellent season. Cool, dry, and clear skies. Avoid October 1–7 (National Day) — the entire country is on the move. The Great Wall and Forbidden City will be unbearably crowded.

❄️ Winter (December–February)

Bitterly cold in the north (Harbin -20°C); mild in the south (Guangzhou 15°C). Best for: Harbin Ice Festival (Jan–Feb), skiing in Hebei, and avoiding crowds at major attractions. Avoid Chinese New Year period (dates vary, typically Jan/Feb) — transportation is chaos.
