Culinary Journey

Taste of the Middle Kingdom

From fiery Sichuan hotpot to delicate Cantonese dim sum — discover China's rich culinary traditions and learn how to eat like a local.

A World of Flavors

Chinese cuisine is as diverse as the country itself. Each of China's 23 provinces has its own unique ingredients, cooking techniques, and signature dishes that tell the story of China's long history and rich culture. What foreigners call "Chinese food" barely scratches the surface of this ancient culinary civilization.

The concept of "Chinese food" in the West — sweet and sour pork, fried rice, chow mein — represents only a tiny fraction of what's actually eaten in China. Real Chinese cuisine is defined by eight great culinary traditions (the "Eight Great Cuisines"), each with distinct flavors, ingredients, and cooking methods shaped by local climate, geography, and history.

Beyond the eight great cuisines, China also has countless local specialties — from the cumin-spiced lamb skewers of Xinjiang to the sour and spicy flavors of Guizhou, from the hearty dumplings of Northeast China to the tropical fruits of Hainan. This guide will help you navigate China's culinary landscape and eat like a local.

The Eight Great Cuisines

These eight regional culinary traditions form the foundation of Chinese cuisine. Each developed over centuries, shaped by local ingredients, climate, and cultural influences.

🌶️

Sichuan (川菜)

Flavor profile: Mala (numbing and spicy), bold, aromatic. Famous for its use of Sichuan peppercorns (huaiao), which create a unique tingling sensation on the tongue.

Key dishes: Mapo tofu, kung pao chicken, twice-cooked pork (huiguo rou), Sichuan hotpot, dandan noodles, fish-fragrant eggplant.

Best place to try: Chengdu, Chongqing

🥟

Cantonese (粤菜)

Flavor profile: Light, fresh, natural, slightly sweet. Emphasis on preserving the ingredient's original flavor. Steaming, stir-frying, and braising are preferred cooking methods.

Key dishes: Dim sum (har gao, siu mai, char siu bao), roast goose, white-cut chicken, steamed fish, wonton noodles.

Best place to try: Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Shenzhen

🦐

Shandong (鲁菜)

Flavor profile: Salty, crispy, fresh, with strong seafood influence. One of the oldest Chinese cuisines, it heavily influenced imperial cuisine during the Ming and Qing dynasties.

Key dishes: Sweet and sour carp, braised abalone, scallion-fried sea cucumber, dezhou braised chicken.

Best place to try: Jinan, Qingdao, Yantai

🐟

Jiangsu (苏菜)

Flavor profile: Fresh, mild, slightly sweet, with meticulous knife skills. Known for elaborate presentation and precise cooking temperatures. Emphasis on soup and broth.

Key dishes: Sweet and sour mandarin fish, Nanjing salted duck, crab roe tofu, lion's head meatballs, Yangzhou fried rice.

Best place to try: Nanjing, Suzhou, Yangzhou

🍃

Zhejiang (浙菜)

Flavor profile: Mellow, fragrant, tender, with emphasis on freshwater fish and bamboo shoots. Similar to Jiangsu but with more seafood influence from the coast.

Key dishes: West Lake vinegar fish, longjing shrimp, beggar's chicken, dongpo pork, crispy eel.

Best place to try: Hangzhou, Ningbo, Shaoxing

🍄

Fujian (闽菜)

Flavor profile: Umami-rich, soupy, with seafood and mushroom dominance. Famous for its complex broths and use of red yeast rice. Light and fresh, never greasy.

Key dishes: Buddha jumps over the wall (fo tiao qiang), lychee pork, oyster omelet, Fujian noodles.

Best place to try: Fuzhou, Xiamen, Quanzhou

🌶️

Hunan (湘菜)

Flavor profile: Hot, sour, and dry-spicy (unlike Sichuan's oily mala). Hunan food uses fresh chilis rather than doubanjiang (fermented bean paste). No numbing sensation.

Key dishes: Chairman Mao's red-braised pork, steamed fish with pickled chilis, hunan rice noodles, stinky tofu.

Best place to try: Changsha, Zhangjiajie

🌿

Anhui (徽菜)

Flavor profile: Rustic, hearty, with wild herbs and mountain ingredients. Less oil, heavy use of cooking wine and sugar. Reflects the rugged Anhui mountain landscape.

Key dishes: Stewed soft-shell turtle with ham, Huangshan braised pigeon, bamboo shoots with sauce, stinky mandarin fish.

Best place to try: Huangshan (Yellow Mountain) area

Must-Try Dishes Across China

These iconic dishes define Chinese cuisine and should be on every traveler's bucket list. Each tells a story of regional culture and history.

🦆

Peking Duck (北京烤鸭)

Originated in Beijing during the Ming Dynasty, this is China's most famous dish. The duck is air-dried, glazed with maltose syrup, and roasted until the skin is crispy and mahogany-colored. Served with thin pancakes, scallions, cucumber, and sweet bean sauce. The best places (Da Dong, Quanjude) have been perfecting this for centuries.

Where to try: Beijing (Da Dong, Quanjude, Siji Minfu)

🥘

Sichuan Hotpot (四川火锅)

A bubbling cauldron of numbingly spicy chili oil broth in the center of the table. You cook your own thinly sliced meats, leafy greens, tofu, and mushrooms. The Yin-Yang (dual) pot offers both spicy and mild broth. Essential accompaniment: sesame oil dipping sauce with garlic.

Where to try: Chengdu, Chongqing (Haidilao, Xiaolongkan)

🥟

Dumplings (饺子)

Dumplings are the soul of Chinese comfort food. Boiled, steamed, pan-fried, or deep-fried — each region has its own style. Jiaozi (boiled dumplings) are essential for Chinese New Year. Xiaolongbao (soup dumplings from Shanghai) require special technique to eat without burning your mouth.

Where to try: Harbin, Tianjin, Shanghai (Din Tai Fung)

🍜

Lanzhou Hand-Pulled Noodles (兰州拉面)

Watch the chef pull the dough into thin strands right before your eyes. Served in a clear beef broth with radish, chili oil, garlic, and coriander. The best bowls have "three thick and three thin" — a reference to the chef's skill in creating varied textures.

Where to try: Lanzhou, but found nationwide

🍵

Dim Sum (点心)

Cantonese "touch the heart" — small, bite-sized portions served with tea. Traditional dim sum is served from steaming carts pushed around the restaurant. Must-tries: har gao (shrimp dumplings), siu mai, char siu bao, egg tarts, and congee.

Where to try: Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Shenzhen

🍖

Dongpo Pork (东坡肉)

Named after the Song Dynasty poet Su Dongpo, this dish features pork belly slow-braised in soy sauce, rice wine, and sugar until melt-in-your-mouth tender. The meat is cut into large cubes, each piece a perfect balance of fat and lean. A true masterpiece of Hangzhou cuisine.

Where to try: Hangzhou (Lou Wai Lou restaurant)

Chinese Dining Etiquette

Understanding dining etiquette will not only make your meal more enjoyable but also show respect for Chinese culture. Here are the essential rules every visitor should know.

🥢 Chopstick Rules

  • Never stick chopsticks vertically into rice — this resembles incense burned for the dead and is extremely bad luck.
  • Don't use chopsticks to point at people or wave them around while talking.
  • Place chopsticks on the chopstick rest (or horizontally across your bowl) when not eating.
  • It's acceptable to lift a bowl to your mouth and push rice in with chopsticks.

🍽️ Seating & Serving

  • The most honored guest sits farthest from the door or facing it.
  • The host typically orders and pays for the meal.
  • Don't start eating until the eldest or most senior person begins.
  • It's polite to serve others (especially elders) before serving yourself.

🍚 Sharing & Sample

  • All dishes are placed in the center and shared by everyone — there is no "your dish."
  • Use the serving spoon or chopsticks (not your own) to take food from communal plates.
  • It's polite to try a little of everything offered to you.
  • Leaving a small amount of food shows you're full (clearing your plate can imply you're still hungry).

💳 Paying the Bill

  • In China, the host always pays — it's a matter of "face" (mianzi).
  • Offering to pay is polite, but the host will usually insist.
  • At casual meals with friends, "going Dutch" (AA) is becoming more common among younger people.
  • Most restaurants accept Alipay and WeChat Pay — cash is rarely used.

How to Order Food in China

📱 Use Translation Apps

Most restaurant menus in China are in Chinese only. Download Google Translate (offline Chinese package) or Pleco before your trip. The camera translation feature can translate a menu in real time. In major cities, some restaurants have English menus — but don't count on it.

🖼️ Point at Pictures

Many restaurants display food photos on the wall or have picture menus. Pointing at a photo and showing it to the server is the universal language of ordering in China. Don't be shy — this is how many locals order too!

📋 Use Food Delivery Apps

Meituan (美团) and Ele.me (饿了么) are China's dominant food delivery platforms. They have English interfaces and offer an incredible variety of food delivered to your hotel in 30 minutes. You'll need a Chinese phone number and Alipay/WeChat Pay set up.

🚨 Tell Them Your Spice Level

Chinese "medium spice" can be very spicy for foreigners. Learn these phrases: "Bu la" (not spicy), "Wei la" (a little spicy), "Zhong la" (medium spicy), "Te la" (very spicy). In Sichuan and Hunan, even "not spicy" dishes may have some heat!
